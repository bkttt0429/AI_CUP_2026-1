# Decision-layer adjustment for ordinal classification under test-time shift (few test labels, custom partial-credit score)

Scope note: notes cover methods that act on stored posteriors (no retraining). Score used in the derivations: exact = 1, adjacent = c = e^-1 ≈ 0.368, else 0. "Validation" = ~33k out-of-fold posteriors; "test" = 800 posteriors (400 public / 400 private). Derivations in the *Inferences* blocks are mine (not from a source) and are marked as such.

## 1. Ordinal regression under shift: threshold re-estimation, ordinal calibration, boundary-specific drift

### Takeaway
I found no paper on boundary-specific drift or label shift *for ordinal* classifiers. The usable machinery is (i) the cumulative-link (proportional-odds) parameterisation, where a shift at one boundary is a single intercept offset, and (ii) general label-shift prior adaptation (Saerens EM + bias-corrected calibration). For a cumulative logit model, a shift at one threshold is algebraically identical to a "single-boundary label shift" (multiply every class on one side of the boundary by the same factor). That gives one principled scalar parameter δ that can be estimated from unlabelled test posteriors plus a few probe labels.

### Cited Findings
- Cumulative link models describe the ordinal response through a latent variable and ordered thresholds that split the real line; P(y ≤ k | x) is modelled through a link function (logit gives proportional odds, probit gives ordered probit). The proportional-odds model (1980) belongs to this family — [Cumulative link models for deep ordinal classification, arXiv 1905.13392](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.13392); [R `ordinal` clm vignette](https://mirror.linux.duke.edu/pub/cran/web/packages/ordinal/vignettes/clm_article.pdf).
- The R `ordinal` package supports partial proportional odds (threshold-specific effects), scale effects and structured thresholds, i.e. per-boundary parameters are a standard modelling device — [clm vignette](https://mirror.linux.duke.edu/pub/cran/web/packages/ordinal/vignettes/clm_article.pdf).
- Searches for "label shift" + ordinal/ordered classes returned only general label-shift work (Saerens 2002 EM; Alexandari et al.; Wen, Betken & Hang ICLR 2024 "Class Probability Matching with Calibrated Networks"; ELSA; a comparison of prior-adaptation methods; a 2026 generalised-label-shift EM paper). None treats ordinal structure — [search summary incl. arXiv 2305.19123](https://pdf.arxiv.org/pdf/2305.19123), [arXiv 2312.07282](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07282v1), [arXiv 2106.11695](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2106.11695), [arXiv 2601.15036](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.15036v2).
- Saerens EM prior adaptation (as written by Alexandari et al.): E-step q(y=i|x_k) = [q(i)/p(i)]·p(i|x_k) / Σ_j [q(j)/p(j)]·p(j|x_k); M-step q(i) = (1/N) Σ_k q(y=i|x_k). It needs only the stored posteriors, cost O(mN) per iteration — [Alexandari, Kundaje, Shrikumar, ICML 2020, arXiv 1901.06852](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.06852).
- The EM (maximum-likelihood) objective in the target priors, Σ_k log Σ_i [p(ω_i|x_k)/p(ω_i)]·q(ω_i), is concave, so EM or any convex solver reaches the global maximum. The likelihood is bounded if every source prior is ≥ ε — [Alexandari et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.06852).
- Calibration matters for prior adaptation. Plain temperature scaling (TS) leaves systematic per-class bias. Bias-corrected TS (BCTS: TS plus per-class bias terms) and vector scaling (VS) reduce it. Source priors must be the *mean calibrated prediction on held-out data*, not label frequencies. EM converges to the source priors iff those coincide (Lemma A). Diabetic-retinopathy example (ρ=0.5, n=500), median accuracy change: no calibration +2.0 (priors from predictions) vs −3.4 (priors from labels); TS +2.2 vs −64.5; BCTS +3.8 vs +3.8 — [Alexandari et al., Table A.1](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.06852).
- Threshold shifting on calibrated probabilities is the standard way to react to changed costs or priors. Elkan argues that changing the training class balance has little effect on Bayesian and tree classifiers and that you should move the decision threshold instead. A 2016 study found that AdaBoost with a shifted threshold and calibrated probabilities was the simplest effective cost-sensitive option — [Elkan, IJCAI 2001](https://mlanthology.org/ijcai/2001/elkan2001ijcai-foundations); [Nikolaou et al., MLJ 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10994-016-5572-x).

### Inferences
- **Single-boundary equivalence (derivation).** Let K = {D,C,B,A} (kyu side) and boundary b = A|1D. Define the label-shift weights w_k(δ) = e^δ for k ∈ K, and 1 for the dan classes. Then the adapted posterior is p'_k ∝ w_k p_k and logit P'(y ≤ A | x) = logit P(y ≤ A | x) + δ for every x. That is exactly a shift of the single cumulative-logit intercept θ_b by δ, and within-side class ratios are unchanged. The shift therefore moves only the decisions at the A|1D boundary. Decisions at B|A, C|B etc. are untouched, apart from second-order partial-credit effects. This is consistent with what you observed: the A|1D push worked, while pushes at other boundaries failed on public. Your current rule ("multiply A by r=2, flip 1D→A only") is a local approximation of this. The cumulative version is cleaner because it never pushes B→A (A and B get the same factor).
- **Label shift vs threshold/labelling shift are not distinguishable from p(y|x) alone, but they differ in p(x).** Under true label shift (more A players in test), the unlabelled marginal changes, and Saerens EM on the 800 unlabelled test posteriors should show an elevated q(kyu)/q(dan) ratio. Under a "labelling-convention" shift (the same players, but the A/1D boundary was graded differently in test), p(x) is unchanged, and EM will show nothing. Only probe labels can identify δ in that case. Running EM restricted to the one-parameter family w(δ) on the 800 posteriors is therefore a cheap **diagnostic of which shift you are facing**.
- **Combined estimator for δ (derivation; uses only stored posteriors).** Let π_k = mean calibrated validation posterior (per Alexandari, Lemma A), U = unlabelled test items, and L = probe-labelled items with labels y_i. Then
  ℓ(δ) = Σ_{i∈U} [ log Σ_k w_k(δ) p_ik − log Σ_j π_j w_j(δ) ] + Σ_{i∈L} log [ w_{y_i}(δ) p_{i,y_i} / Σ_k w_k(δ) p_ik ] + log prior(δ).
  It is one-dimensional, so evaluate it on a grid. The probe term is a valid conditional likelihood because the probed items were selected on x (the posterior odds), not on y. The prior on δ should come from validation: fit δ per CV fold or per data sub-period on validation and use the spread of those per-fold δ̂ as σ_δ.
- **Ordinal calibration before adaptation.** The cited evidence favours a per-class-bias calibrator (BCTS/VS) fitted on the 33k OOF posteriors before any prior adaptation. For ordinal outputs, the natural analogue is a per-boundary bias on the cumulative logits ("cumulative BCTS": one temperature plus 9 boundary offsets). This is my suggestion; I found no paper evaluating it.

- No paper found on per-boundary drift detection or per-boundary threshold re-estimation for ordinal classifiers under shift. The equivalence above is a derivation, not a cited result.
- No source found on "ordinal calibration" methods specifically (e.g. per-boundary temperature). Searches returned only general calibration (TS/VS/BCTS).

## 2. Ordinal quantification: methods, benchmarks, and whether ~800 points suffice

### Takeaway
Ordinal quantification (OQ) methods estimate the test class-prevalence vector, not per-item decisions. The best published OQ methods are ordinally regularised versions of SLD/EM (o-SLD) and of probabilistic adjusted classify-and-count (o-PACC). They reach NMD ≈ 0.01–0.02 on samples of around a thousand items. That is good enough to detect gross prevalence shifts but probably not a single-boundary shift of a few percentage points with 800 items. Quantification also does not estimate per-boundary *confusion* (p(y|x)) changes, which is what the A|1D anomaly may be.

### Cited Findings
- Bunse, Moreo, Sebastiani & Senz, "Regularization-Based Methods for Ordinal Quantification" (arXiv 2310.09210; Data Mining & Knowledge Discovery 2024; earlier ECML-PKDD 2022 version "Ordinal Quantification through Regularization"). It contributes two OQ datasets, a comparison of the main OQ algorithms, and a family of ordinally regularised methods — [arXiv 2310.09210](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.09210?context=cs); [Lamarr Institute page](https://lamarr-institute.org/publication/regularization-based-methods-for-ordinal-quantification).
- Formulation: many quantifiers solve q = M p. For ACC, M_ij = fraction of validation items of true class j that the classifier assigns to class i, q = the predicted-class distribution on the test sample, and the estimate minimises ||q − M p||². Ordinal regularisation adds a Tikhonov term (τ/2)·||C p||², where C has second-difference rows (−1, 2, −1) that penalise "humps". The simplex is enforced by p̂ = softmax(l) with l_1 = 0 — [Bunse et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.09210).
- Evaluation metric: NMD(p, p̂) = MD(p, p̂)/(n−1), with MD = Σ_{i=1}^{n−1} |P̂(y_i) − P(y_i)| over cumulative distributions (adjacent distance 1). It ranges from 0 to 1. MD is a special case of the Earth Mover's Distance — [Bunse et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.09210).
- Smoothness assumption: jaggedness ξ₁(p) = (1/min(6, n+1)) Σ (−p_{i−1} + 2p_i − p_{i+1})². Real distributions are smooth (Amazon categories ≈ 0.007–0.037; FACT ≈ 0.0115). Their APP-OQ protocol keeps only the smoothest x% of sampled prevalence vectors (50% Amazon, 5% FACT) — [Bunse et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.09210).
- Headline NMD results (lower is better). Amazon-OQ-BK (5 classes) under APP / APP-OQ / real prevalences: CC .0534/.0434/.0295; PCC .0611/.0496/.0319; PACC .0289/.0243/.0164; SLD .0217/.0200/.0145; **o-SLD .0194/.0190/.0145**. Fact-OQ (12 classes) APP / APP-OQ / real: SLD .0192/.0162/.0126; o-SLD .0187/.0150/.0118; **o-PACC .0196/.0139/.0095**. The authors state that "the ordinally regularized variant is always better than or equal to the original" — [Bunse et al., Tables 3–4](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.09210).
- PCC does worse with ordinal classifiers, which "suggests that the posterior probabilities computed under the ordinal assumption are of lower quality" — [Bunse et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.09210).
- Sample size: one reading of the paper reported validation/test samples of 1,000 items (training 20,000; 1,000 validation and 5,000 test samples), while a second pass over the results section said the paper does not state a per-sample size there and that APP protocols "require huge datasets". Treat the "1,000 items per sample" figure as unverified — [Bunse et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.09210).
- Castaño, González, González & del Coz, "Matching Distributions Algorithms Based on the Earth Mover's Distance for Ordinal Quantification", IEEE TNNLS (cited as 2022 by quantificationlib, 2023/2024 elsewhere). It is implemented in `quantificationlib`, which also provides `emd(p_true, p_pred)` and `emd_score` — [quantificationlib ordinal metrics](https://aicgijon.github.io/quantificationlib/rsts_t4/quantificationlib.metrics.ordinal.html); [Bunse et al. citing Castaño et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.09210).
- LeQua 2024 Task T3 is an ordinal quantification task: Amazon reviews with 1–5 star classes, prior-probability shift, NMD as the official measure, training bags of 200 items for T3. Results were presented at the LQ 2024 workshop — [LeQua 2024 tasks](https://lequa2024.github.io/tasks); [LeQua 2024 dataset on Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/11091067); [LQ 2024 workshop](https://lequa2024.github.io/workshop). The Lamarr team reports 2nd place in T3 (and 1st in T4) — [Lotz et al., LQ 2024 proceedings](https://lq-2024.github.io/proceedings/Lotz2024.pdf).
- On label-shift weight estimation at small n (diabetic retinopathy, 5 classes, strong Dirichlet shift ρ=0.5), median MSE of the estimated weights: EM (no calibration) 0.351 at n=500 and 0.305 at n=1000; EM+BCTS 0.402 and 0.248; best RLLS 0.919 and 0.635; best BBSE 1.404 and 0.713. At n=500, uncalibrated EM was slightly better than the bias-corrected variants. At n ≥ 1000, BCTS/VS were best — [Alexandari et al., Table 4](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.06852).

### Inferences
- **Precision with 800 items (napkin).** The sampling SE of a class share near 10% at n=800 is √(0.1·0.9/800) ≈ 0.011. On top of that comes method error. Alexandari's n=500–1000 weight MSE of 0.25–0.40 (RMSE ≈ 0.5–0.6 on weights near 1) is for 5 classes; 10 classes will be worse. A full 10-class prior vector from 800 test posteriors is therefore too noisy to drive decisions directly. Restrict the shift to 1–2 parameters (the single-boundary δ in §1), or use o-SLD's smoothness penalty, before using it for decisions.
- **What OQ can and cannot tell you.** o-SLD/o-PACC on the 800 test posteriors can test "is the kyu/dan mass split different from validation?" (a label shift). They cannot see a change in where the organisers put the A|1D boundary for the same kind of players (a concept shift). If o-SLD shows no kyu/dan shift while probes show 8/8 A, the evidence points to concept/label-noise shift, and probes are the only information source.
- **Per-boundary confusion.** The ACC/PACC matrix M is estimated on validation and assumed fixed. That is exactly the assumption the A|1D anomaly violates. OQ therefore does not estimate per-boundary confusion under shift. Probe labels are needed to update the one column/row of M involved.

- Official LeQua 2024 T3 leaderboard numbers (winner, NMD values) were not found. The results page and the overview paper were not retrieved.
- No OQ study found that reports accuracy specifically at n ≈ 800 with 10 classes.
- The Castaño et al. EMD-matching paper's algorithms and results were not retrieved (only metadata and library docs).

## 3. Transductive decision rules: quota-constrained assignment / optimal transport; gains and risks when counts are wrong

### Takeaway
Quota-constrained assignment (an LP or Hungarian matching to class counts) is mathematically equivalent to choosing one additive bias per class (the LP dual variables) so that the argmax produces the target counts. In other words, it is a count-calibrated version of the "bias-only push" you already use. The published OT label-allocation methods use proportions as *upper-bound* constraints during self-training, not as hard quotas at decision time. For an expected-score objective, forcing predicted counts to equal true counts is generally *not* optimal and is fragile to count error.

### Cited Findings
- Sinkhorn Label Allocation (Tai, Bailis, Valiant, ICML 2021) casts pseudo-label assignment as optimal transport between examples and classes, with class-proportion priors as **upper-bound** constraints, solved approximately by Sinkhorn iterations inside training. It was evaluated on CIFAR-10/100 and SVHN against FixMatch — [PMLR v139 tai21a](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v139/tai21a.html).
- In learning from label proportions, Liu et al. (2021) used OT to get pseudo-labels that meet *exact* proportions, and SwAV uses an equipartition OT constraint (a variant swaps in exact cluster proportions) — [AAAI 2022 paper citing them](https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/20112/20112-13-24125-1-2-20220628.pdf).
- Prior-shift EM (the soft alternative to quotas) adapts posteriors by the estimated prior ratio and then decides per item. With calibrated inputs it beat BBSE/RLLS in weight MSE and downstream accuracy (e.g. +3.8 / +4.65 accuracy points for EM+BCTS at n=500 / 1000, ρ=0.5) — [Alexandari et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.06852).

### Inferences
- **Algorithm (derivation).** Let S_ik = expected score of answering k for item i = p_ik + c(p_i,k−1 + p_i,k+1). Given counts n_k with Σ n_k = 800, solve max Σ_ik x_ik S_ik subject to Σ_k x_ik = 1 and Σ_i x_ik = n_k, with x ≥ 0. This is a transportation LP with integral vertices, so the solution is a hard assignment. It can be solved with `scipy.optimize.linprog`, or with `linear_sum_assignment` on an 800×800 matrix that repeats column k n_k times. A soft (entropic) version is Sinkhorn on exp(S/ε). The LP dual gives per-class offsets λ_k, and the optimum is "argmax_k (S_ik + λ_k)". **So quota matching = choosing 10 class biases to hit target counts.**
- **Why hard quotas are usually wrong for this score (derivation).** With correct posteriors, the per-item argmax of S_ik is already Bayes-optimal. Its predicted-count vector is *not* the true count vector, because partial credit rewards hedging toward middle classes and uncertain items get pulled inward. Forcing predicted counts to equal true counts therefore moves uncertain items toward the extremes and lowers expected score, even when n_k is exactly right. The correct transductive use of counts is to **reweight posteriors** (p'_ik ∝ (q_k/π_k) p_ik, Saerens) and then take the per-item argmax of the expected score. Do not match counts.
- **Gain if counts were known (rough).** Under pure label shift the gain equals that of using the true prior ratio in Saerens adaptation. For your case, only the A|1D boundary is affected. One flipped item is worth ±(1−c)/400 = ±0.00158 on a 400-question board (+ when the truth is A, − when it is 1D; ±c/400 when the truth is B or 2D). Your public +0.0126 ≈ 8 × 0.00158, i.e. all 8 flips correct. The expected private gain is (number of private flips) × (2·precision − 1) × 0.00158 (ignoring B/2D outcomes).
- **Risks.** (1) Count mis-estimation: with an LP, every unit of count error forces one item across a boundary. The items moved are the least certain ones, and with 800 items and count SEs of about ±9 per class (from the SE above), noise-driven flips would swamp an 8-item effect. (2) The quota is global. It moves items at *all* boundaries to satisfy counts, re-introducing the B|A and other pushes that failed on public. (3) Concept shift: if the anomaly is a boundary relabelling rather than a prior change, true counts may not differ from what the posteriors imply, and quotas give no signal.
- **Entropy-minimisation TTA** (TENT-style) updates model parameters (e.g. normalisation layers) on test inputs. It is not applicable to stored posteriors. The stored-posterior analogue is the prior EM above. I did not retrieve TENT sources in this session.

### Gaps
- No empirical study found that quantifies accuracy gains or losses of hard quota assignment versus prior-reweighting under count mis-estimation for ordinal data.
- No sources retrieved on TENT / entropy-minimisation TTA risks (collapse, confirmation bias).

## 4. Robust decisions under prior uncertainty for a custom ordinal score (minimax regret, Bayes with uncertain priors)

### Takeaway
Distributionally robust label-shift work (Zhang et al., ICLR 2021) is training-time: it minimises worst-case risk over a set of test priors. For stored posteriors, the practical analogue is cheap and exact. Treat the boundary shift δ (or r = e^δ) as uncertain with a posterior built from validation spread plus probe labels. Then either (Bayes) average the adapted expected scores over δ, or (robust) flip only items whose flip is non-negative across a credible interval of δ. With a one-dimensional δ, both reduce to a per-item odds threshold.

### Cited Findings
- Zhang et al., "Coping with Label Shift via Distributionally Robust Optimisation" (ICLR 2021). It minimises worst-case expected risk over test label distributions in an uncertainty set. The motivation is that an adversary can place large weight on classes with high per-class loss. Optimisation is by gradient descent–proximal mirror ascent. Related DRO formulations use an f-divergence ball around the source prior — [arXiv 2010.12230](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.12230); [ICLR 2021 poster](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2021/poster/3242); [emergentmind label-shift summary](https://api.emergentmind.com/topics/label-shift).
- Minimax rates for label shift: distribution-matching approaches are minimax rate-optimal when target data cannot be used to estimate class-conditionals — [Maity, Sun & Banerjee, arXiv 2003.10443](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.10443v1).
- Priors and misclassification costs enter the Bayes decision rule jointly. Shifting the decision threshold is the cost-optimal adjustment for a calibrated classifier — [Elkan 2001](https://mlanthology.org/ijcai/2001/elkan2001ijcai-foundations); multi-class rescaling needs extra care — [AAAI 2006 multi-class rescaling paper](https://fileadmin.cs.lth.se/ai/Proceedings/aaai06/05/AAAI06-091.pdf).

### Inferences (derivations; not from sources)
- **Exact flip condition for this score.** Answer A instead of 1D iff S_A − S_1D = (1−c)(p'_A − p'_1D) + c(p'_B − p'_2D) > 0, where p' are adapted posteriors. With the single-boundary adaptation p'_k ∝ r·p_k for k ≤ A, the condition is (1−c)(r p_A − p_1D) + c(r p_B − p_2D) > 0. Ignoring the B/2D term, it is the odds rule p_A/p_1D > 1/r. So "r = 2 ⇔ flip at odds ≥ 1/2", consistent with your rule.
- **How strong is 8/8?** If the flipped-set precision on test equalled validation (0.47), P(8/8) = 0.47^8 ≈ 0.0024. Even with a Bonferroni factor of about 10 for the boundary pushes tried on public, p ≈ 0.02. So the shift is probably real. Under the label-shift reading, a rule-set item with validation precision p has test precision w·p / (w·p + 1 − p). For p = 0.47 that gives 0.64 (w=2), 0.78 (w=4) and 0.88 (w=8), with P(8/8) = 0.028, 0.137 and 0.347 respectively. So 8/8 favours w well above 2: a likelihood ratio of about 57 for w=4 versus w=1. Under a pure label-shift model the optimal push r equals w, so **r = 2 is, if anything, conservative**. The cost of a larger r is that it flips items with lower base odds (1/4 ≤ odds < 1/2), whose precision has not been tested.
- **Bayes-with-uncertain-prior decision (algorithm).** (1) Prior on δ = log r: N(0, σ_δ²), with σ_δ from the per-fold spread of the A|1D intercept on validation (§1). (2) Posterior on δ ∝ prior × probe likelihood (Bernoulli terms from the 8 labels, plus any more probes) × optionally the unlabelled EM term. (3) For each test item, flip iff E_δ[S_A(δ) − S_1D(δ)] > 0, computed by averaging over a δ grid. Because the expected score is linear in the adapted posterior, this is the Bayes-optimal rule under δ-uncertainty.
- **Expected private value (illustrative).** Use a Beta prior on the test flipped-set precision centred on the validation value 0.47. With a loose prior (sd 0.15 → Beta(4.7, 5.3)), the posterior after 8/8 is Beta(12.7, 5.3) with mean ≈ 0.705. That is worth (2·0.705 − 1) × 0.632 ≈ +0.26 score units per flip, i.e. ≈ +0.00065 per flip on a 400-question board → ≈ +0.005 for about 8 private flips. With a tight prior that trusts validation (sd 0.05 → Beta(46, 52)), the posterior mean is ≈ 0.51 and the gain is ≈ +0.0002 total. **The private EV is dominated by the prior on how much the test boundary can move.** That should be estimated empirically from validation sub-period/fold variation of δ̂, not chosen by hand.
- **Minimax-regret alternative.** Take a credible interval [δ_lo, δ_hi]. Flip item i only if S_A − S_1D ≥ 0 at δ_lo (robust: never worse under any δ in the set). Minimax-regret instead picks the flip set minimising the maximum, over δ in the interval, of (best achievable at δ − achieved), which for a 1-D δ is a threshold between the two endpoints' rules. Robust rules are justified when the δ posterior is wide and the private board is the only one that counts.
- **Value of further probes.** Probing items in the next odds band (1/4 ≤ p_A/p_1D < 1/2) directly tests whether r should exceed 2. Probing more items in the current band (odds ≥ 1/2) mainly narrows precision that is already high. Under a label-shift model the information per probe is highest where p'_A is near 0.5 under the current δ posterior.

## 5. Competition evidence: post-hoc ordinal threshold optimisation and public→private transfer

### Takeaway
Kaggle ordinal competitions (APTOS 2019, PetFinder 2019, Prudential) routinely used post-hoc thresholds tuned for QWK (OptimizedRounder / Nelder–Mead). The write-ups I could reach report unstable CV ↔ public ↔ private correlation and large public→private swings, i.e. thresholds and boundary pushes tuned on a small public split often do not transfer. I could not retrieve the winning write-ups themselves.

### Cited Findings
- APTOS 2019 (diabetic retinopathy, 5 ordinal grades, QWK). A 28th-place write-up tuned thresholds with QWK optimisation (Nelder–Mead). It reported inconsistent correlation between local CV, public and private, called threshold-based confusion-matrix metrics unstable, and had private 0.925 vs public 0.832. Another participant with manually tuned thresholds reported public 0.804 vs private 0.924 — [APTOS 2019 28th place (Speaker Deck)](https://speakerdeck.com/hoxomaxwell/aptos-2019-28th-place-solution); [APTOS 2019 48th place (Speaker Deck)](https://speakerdeck.com/icebee16/aptos2019-48th-solution). (Seen via search snippets, not full decks; attribution of the second figure between the decks is uncertain.)
- In the Kaggle ASAP short-answer scoring competition, participants could submit twice a day for two months, which gave "ample opportunity" to fit parameters to the public leaderboard; an independent private test set existed — [short-answer scoring paper, arXiv 1403.0801](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1403.0801).
- A PetFinder write-up converts class probabilities to an expected value and splits it with four QWK-optimised cut-points. It warns that fitting cut-points on validation folds leaks and that production cut-points should be fit out-of-fold — [Towards AI PetFinder write-up](https://pub.towardsai.net/multimodal-pet-adoption-speed-prediction-with-an-agentic-ai-layer-1a5b932012b7).
- Direct QWK maximisation has had little formal study (Vaughn & Justice's derivation was retracted) — [arXiv 1612.00775 appendix](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1612.00775); [arXiv 1509.07107](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.07107). A 2026 AIMECON paper comparing seven cut-point methods notes that cut-points are rarely treated as a first-class modelling decision — [AIMECON 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.aimecon-main.41.pdf).

### Inferences
- These competitions differ from yours. Their thresholds were tuned on a public score without item-level label knowledge, and with QWK, which is far noisier per threshold than your exact/adjacent score. Your A|1D push is supported by item-level evidence (8/8 known labels), not just an aggregate public delta. The Kaggle shake-up evidence is a warning against *aggregate-score-tuned* pushes (your failed boundary pushes are an instance). It is weaker evidence against a push validated item by item.
- Public and private are presumably random halves of one 800-question test pool. If so, a boundary shift that is real on public applies equally to private, and the shake-up risk reduces to the sampling noise of the roughly 8 private items in the band. If the split is not random (e.g. by time or player), transfer is not guaranteed.

### Gaps
- Winning write-ups for APTOS, PetFinder (rumoured "distribution-matched" rounding to the train class distribution) and Prudential were not retrieved. I cannot confirm whether quota/distribution-matched thresholds helped or hurt on private.
- No systematic study found of how often tuned ordinal thresholds transfer public → private.
